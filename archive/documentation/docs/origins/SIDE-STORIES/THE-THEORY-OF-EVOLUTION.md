# SIDE STORY: THE THEORY OF EVOLUTION

I was looking at an old project.

Not intentionally. I was cleaning up, archiving files, trying to make sense of years of accumulated work. And I found it—a project from the beginning, when I was still figuring things out.

The code was messy. The architecture was wrong. The documentation was incomplete. The decisions I had made felt naive, in retrospect. Obvious mistakes that I would never make now.

I could see the evolution clearly.

Each project had learned from the previous one. Not intentionally. Not systematically. Just through the accumulation of experience—the failures that taught me what didn't work, the successes that showed me what did.

Somewhere in that accumulation, the methodology had changed.

---

I don't remember when I first started thinking about evolution.

Not in the biological sense. Not about Darwin or natural selection or any of the details. Just the general idea: systems change over time, and some changes survive while others disappear.

I had been building things for years. Software, processes, methodologies. Each one different. Each one informed by what came before.

And I started noticing something.

The projects that worked shared certain characteristics. They made assumptions explicit. They tested those assumptions. They kept track of what they learned.

The projects that failed shared different characteristics. They assumed they knew the answer. They built without testing. They forgot what they learned.

The difference was not talent. The difference was method.

---

I tried to remember the first project.

There were so many since then. Different technologies, different teams, different problems. Each one felt different in the moment. But looking back, the pattern emerged.

The methodology was evolving.

Early projects were guesswork. Build something, hope it works, fix what breaks. Later projects were more structured. Ask questions first. Test assumptions. Keep track of what you learn.

I did not plan this evolution. Nobody told me to do it. It just happened—because the unstructured approach kept failing, and the structured approach kept working.

Somewhere in the middle, I started writing things down.

Not because I thought documentation mattered. Just because I kept forgetting. Kept making the same mistakes. Kept losing track of what I had learned.

The notes accumulated. The notes became processes. The processes became methodology.

I had not designed a methodology. I had evolved one.

---

I read about evolution, eventually.

Not to learn about biology. To understand the mechanism. To see if what I was observing had a name.

Variation. Selection. Inheritance.

Systems change randomly. Some changes survive. Successful changes pass to the next generation.

This was what was happening to my methodology.

Each project was a variation. Some variations worked better than others. The successful ones were retained. The failures were discarded. The next project inherited the accumulated wisdom of all previous projects.

I had not planned this. I had just kept building. And somewhere in the building, the methodology had improved itself.

---

The question that came then was strange:

Can this happen intentionally?

Not by accident. Not through accumulated experience. But through deliberate practice of the same mechanisms.

Variation. Selection. Inheritance.

What if I designed experiments to create variation? What if I built systems to select what works? What if I made sure successful practices were inherited by the next project?

I did not know if it would work. The biological analogy felt imperfect. Engineering is not biology.

But the core idea seemed right: systems that improve over time do so because they can experiment, learn, and retain what works.

What would such a system look like?

---

I don't remember when I first used the word investigation.

It came naturally. You investigate a question. You gather evidence. You form hypotheses and test them.

The language fit. And somewhere in using the language, the structure emerged.

Observe. What is actually happening?

Hypothesize. What might explain it?

Experiment. Try the change.

Evidence. What did we learn?

Revise. Update our understanding.

The pattern was so familiar it felt obvious. The way you learn anything.

And then I thought: this is evolution. Not metaphorically. Structurally.

The same mechanisms. Variation in what we try. Selection based on evidence. Inheritance of what works.

I had been running experiments without calling them experiments. Testing hypotheses without using that word. Evolving my methodology without recognizing the process.

---

The realization did not change what I did.

It changed how I understood what I was doing.

I had been evolving. Not just building things—building a way of building things. Each project inherited something from the previous one. Each failure taught something that the next project used.

The methodology was not a fixed thing. It was a living system, changing over time, shaped by continuous selection.

Every investigation was an experiment. Every validated principle was an inherited trait. Every failed assumption was an extinction.

KDE was not designed. KDE was evolved.

---

I think about this now, when I look at the methodology.

It is not perfect. It is not complete. It will continue to change, to adapt, to evolve.

Some practices will survive. Some will disappear. The ones that remain will be the ones that work—the ones that help us discover truth, that test our assumptions, that keep us from fooling ourselves.

This is not a flaw. This is the mechanism.

Evolution does not produce perfect systems. It produces systems that work well enough to survive.

And then they keep working. Keep adapting. Keep becoming more capable over time.

The methodology is not finished. It will never be finished.

But it is better than it was. And it will be better than it is now.

Because the same process that created it continues to shape it.

Variation. Selection. Inheritance.

Reality continuously selecting better engineering ideas.

---

I look back at that old project sometimes.

The messy code. The wrong architecture. The naive decisions.

And I think: that project did not fail. It taught me something. Its failures became the variation that the next project selected from.

Every project is part of the lineage. Every lesson is inherited.

The methodology is not separate from the work.

The methodology is the work, accumulated over time.

And like every living system, it remains unfinished.

Not because it is incomplete.

Because evolution never truly ends.
