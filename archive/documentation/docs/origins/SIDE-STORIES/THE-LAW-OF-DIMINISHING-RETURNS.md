# SIDE STORY: THE LAW OF DIMINISHING RETURNS

I was running the same test again.

Not for the first time. Not for the second. I had lost count of how many times I had run it.

The test always passed. The evidence was always the same. The understanding was always confirmed.

And yet I kept running it.

I could not explain why. The test had never revealed anything new. Each run produced the same result. Each iteration said the same thing in slightly different words.

I was collecting confirmation when I already had certainty.

---

I had been working on the same investigation for weeks.

The early work was productive. Each experiment removed a major uncertainty. Each piece of evidence clarified something I had not understood before.

The second phase was still valuable. The evidence refined what I had learned. The picture became sharper.

By the third phase, the picture was mostly complete. The remaining uncertainty was small. The evidence was strong.

And then I kept going.

I don't know why. Habit, maybe. Thoroughness. The feeling that more evidence was always better than less.

But the additional evidence mostly said what the existing evidence already said.

---

I thought about the first experiment.

How much uncertainty it had removed. How much I had learned. How the evidence had changed my understanding dramatically.

The tenth experiment removed almost no uncertainty at all.

The effort was the same. The result was different.

The first work was discovery. The later work was confirmation.

Both had value. But the value was not proportional to the effort.

---

I thought about the law of diminishing returns.

Not from a textbook. Not from a course. Just the general idea: at some point, additional input stops producing proportional output.

The idea seemed obvious in economics. You add fertilizer to a field. The first additions increase yield significantly. Later additions increase yield less and less. Eventually, adding more fertilizer decreases yield.

Why should engineering be different?

---

The early work always removes the most uncertainty.

The first experiment tests the central hypothesis. It reveals whether the basic assumption is right or wrong. It changes everything or nothing.

The second experiment refines that finding. The third narrows it further.

Each experiment adds less uncertainty reduction than the one before.

By the time you have run ten experiments, the next experiment adds almost nothing.

The law is not specific to any domain. It is a general pattern of how evidence accumulates.

---

I don't remember when I first recognized this.

Maybe it was when I stopped running a test and felt no loss. Because the test had nothing new to say.

Maybe it was when I looked at an investigation and realized: the important work was done. The remaining questions were small. The effort required to answer them would exceed the value of the answers.

Maybe it was just something that accumulated, the way recognition does.

What I remember is the feeling: more work does not always mean more knowledge.

---

The problem was never working hard.

The problem was continuing long after the important discoveries had already been made.

The first experiment eliminated major uncertainty. The second refined it. The third strengthened confidence.

The tenth repeated what was already known.

The effort was constant. The discovery was not.

---

I started asking a different question.

Not "how can I be more thorough?" Not "what else should I test?"

But "when is this investigation complete?"

The question felt strange. Investigations were supposed to continue until you had answers. Until you understood. Until the work was done.

But when was the work done?

When the remaining uncertainty no longer justified the effort required to reduce it.

When another experiment would mostly confirm what you already knew.

When the question you were asking was more valuable than another answer to a question you had already answered.

---

The engineer's responsibility is not to pursue endless investigation.

It is to recognize when evidence has become sufficient.

This is not laziness. This is not stopping early. This is judgment.

Knowing when to continue and when to stop. Knowing when refinement matters and when it does not. Knowing when the next question is more valuable than another answer to the current one.

These are engineering skills as much as debugging or architecture design.

---

I think about this now, when I start an investigation.

Not to stop early. Not to skip important work.

But to recognize when the important work is done.

The first experiments remove the most uncertainty. The later experiments confirm it.

At some point, the confirmation becomes repetition. The refinement becomes overengineering. The verification becomes ritual.

That point is not fixed. It depends on the question, the stakes, the remaining uncertainty.

But it exists.

And recognizing it is part of the work.

---

Discovery has a point of diminishing returns.

The engineer's job is not to investigate forever.

It is to know when the investigation has taught what it needed to teach.

And when it is time to ask the next question.

---

I still run thorough experiments.

I still collect evidence.

But I watch for the moment when the evidence stops telling me something new.

And when that moment comes, I have learned something important:

The work is not measured by how much you do.

It is measured by what you learn.

And sometimes, the most valuable thing you learn is that you have learned enough.
