# SIDE STORY: THE ESCALATOR

I was waiting at the bottom of an escalator.

Not waiting to ride—just waiting. Someone had stopped ahead of me in the corridor, and I had nowhere to go. So I watched.

The escalator was going up. People approached from behind me, stepped on, rode, and stepped off at the top. One after another, like a conveyor belt of human movement. The escalator didn't care who was riding. It just moved.

What I noticed was the difference in how people stepped on.

Some people stepped on without slowing down. They reached the edge, placed a foot, found their balance, and kept moving. They were already thinking about something else—checking their phone, talking to a companion, planning the next thing. The stepping was automatic. It required nothing from them.

Other people slowed down. They looked at the steps. They watched other people step on first. They seemed to be calculating something—where exactly to place their foot, how much space they needed, whether the step would be there when they stepped. Sometimes they stepped on successfully and rode away. Sometimes they hesitated at the edge for a moment longer than necessary, creating a small gap behind them.

The gap was the interesting part.

---

I started watching for it after that.

At every escalator, I looked for the moment where someone would slow down, calculate, and create a gap. It happened often enough that I began to notice patterns. First-time visitors to the city hesitated more than locals. People carrying bags hesitated more than people with empty hands. People in their sixties and seventies hesitated more than people in their twenties.

And there was another pattern, though it took me longer to see it: sometimes experienced users would hesitate too.

Not because they didn't know how to use an escalator. They knew perfectly well. But something would make them think about it—maybe a new kind of shoe, maybe a crowded platform, maybe just a moment of distraction. And in that moment, they would slow down. They would look. They would calculate.

They would try to do it perfectly.

---

There's a word for what happens when you try to do an automatic thing perfectly. It's called the centipede effect.

The story goes like this: a centipede walks happily along until someone asks it which leg moves after which. It thinks about it. It tries to figure out the order of its legs. And then it can't walk anymore.

I've read this story in psychology books. The psychologists call it hyperreflection—conscious attention to a task that has become automatic. When you learn a skill, you practice it until it becomes unconscious. You don't have to think about it. You just do it.

But if you start thinking about it—if you try to do it consciously, to optimize it, to do it perfectly—the skill breaks down. The thinking interferes with the doing.

The centipede effect.

I didn't know this word when I was watching the escalator. But I think I was seeing it.

---

The person who hesitates isn't just nervous or inexperienced. They're trying to do it right.

They want to step in the center of the step. They want to time it perfectly so their foot lands exactly when the step arrives. They want to avoid stumbling. They want to be graceful.

All of these goals require conscious attention. And conscious attention is slow.

An automatic action takes almost no time. The brain sends a signal, the body responds, done. A conscious action takes longer because the brain has to process, decide, and then send the signal.

So the person who tries to step perfectly actually steps slower. The person who doesn't think about it steps faster.

This is counterintuitive. You'd expect careful to be fast. But careful requires thought. Thought requires time. Time creates gaps.

---

I think about the centipede often now.

The centipede didn't forget how to walk. It just started thinking about walking. The thinking broke the walking.

Sometimes, later, when you're building something—a system, a process, a way of doing things—you find yourself thinking about the gaps. About the hesitation. About why people slow down when they should flow.

You don't remember where the thought came from. But it's there, somewhere in the back of your mind: the escalator, the gaps, the people who tried too hard.

You start asking questions without realizing you're asking them. Why does this feel slow? Why does this require thought? What would make it automatic?

The answers come easier than they should. Because somewhere in your memory, there's an escalator. There's the observation. There's the pattern you noticed and didn't know you'd remember.

The centipede didn't forget how to walk.

It just started thinking about walking.

And when you design something and it works—really works, the way an escalator works, without hesitation, without gaps—you're not thinking about the escalator. You're not thinking about any of it.

But your mind is.

---

The escalator kept moving while I waited.

People stepped on. People stepped off. Some hesitated. Most didn't.

The ones who hesitated weren't confused. They were trying too hard.

I don't remember when the observation became useful. Memory doesn't work that way. It gives you fragments, and later—much later—you find the fragments fitting together.

But somewhere in the back of my mind, there's still the escalator. The gaps. The people who tried to do it perfectly and made it slower.

And when something works without hesitation, without gaps, without that moment where someone has to stop and think—

I don't remember why.

But I notice.
