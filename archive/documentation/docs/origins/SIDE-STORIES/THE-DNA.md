# SIDE STORY: THE DNA

I was in a biology class.

Not paying attention, really. It was the kind of class you sit through without expecting anything to stick. The teacher was talking about cells, about molecules, about things too small to see. The diagrams on the board looked like abstract art—spirals and letters that meant nothing to me.

But then I heard the numbers.

Four letters. That was what the teacher said. Four letters—A, C, G, T—and everything alive on Earth is written in them.

I looked at the diagram again. The spiral, the pairs, the ladder that seemed too simple to contain anything real. And I thought: that's it? That's all there is?

---

Four letters.

I thought about alphabets. English has twenty-six letters. Other languages have more, or fewer, but usually in the same range. Twenty-six to write everything—love letters, laws, the complete works of Shakespeare.

But four. Only four.

The teacher explained it slowly, like we were supposed to understand. Three letters make a codon. Each codon specifies an amino acid. Twenty amino acids, plus signals for start and stop. Sixty-four combinations from four letters taken three at a time.

Sixty-four.

I tried to do the math in my head. Four letters. Combinations of three. Four times four times four. That was all.

And from that—all of life. Every tree, every insect, every person who ever lived. All written in the same four-letter alphabet.

---

I didn't think about it for years after that.

The class ended. I moved on to other things. I forgot the teacher's name, the diagram, most of what was said.

But I remembered the numbers.

Four letters. Sixty-four combinations. Twenty amino acids.

And the question that came with them: how does something so small contain something so vast?

---

Years later, I was working on something—I don't remember what anymore. Some problem about representing information. About how to take complex things and describe them simply. About how to store knowledge in a form that computers could use.

The problem was always the same. The information was too large. The descriptions were too long. Every attempt at compression lost something important. Every attempt at preservation required too much space.

I was frustrated. I was stuck.

And then, somewhere in the middle of the frustration, I remembered the four letters.

---

I started thinking about DNA again.

Not the biology—I didn't care about the biology. I cared about the principle.

Four symbols. Sixty-four combinations. Infinite variety.

How?

The answer was structure. The four letters weren't random. They were organized. Three letters meant one thing. Position mattered. Context mattered. The same three letters in different places meant different things.

The information wasn't compressed by making things shorter. It was compressed by making things systematic. By creating rules that allowed a small alphabet to represent a vast space of possibilities.

I started asking different questions.

What if our knowledge could work the same way? What if we didn't need thousands of different symbols, but only a few, organized systematically? What if meaning came from structure, not just from content?

---

I don't remember the exact moment the idea became useful.

Memory doesn't work that way. It gives you fragments—the feeling of understanding, the question that stayed with you, the pattern that seemed to fit.

What I remember is that I started seeing the world differently. Not in DNA, exactly. But in systems. In structures. In the way small, consistent elements could combine to create vast, complex, meaningful wholes.

Four letters. All of life.

A few symbols. All of knowledge.

The encoding wasn't the information. The encoding was a system for representing information. The power came not from the symbols themselves but from their organization.

---

I think about that class sometimes.

The teacher who didn't know I was listening. The diagram I didn't understand. The numbers that stuck when everything else faded.

Four letters.

It's such a small thing to carry with you. Such an ordinary fact—something anyone who ever took a biology class might know.

But it changed how I thought about everything after.

Not because DNA is special. But because it showed me something about systems, about encoding, about how complexity emerges from simplicity.

The complete works of Shakespeare, written in twenty-six letters. All of life, written in four.

The alphabet doesn't matter. What matters is what you do with it.

---

I don't know if I ever actually saw a diagram of DNA that day in class. I don't know if the teacher really said those numbers, or if I've filled in the gaps over the years.

Memory is like that. It keeps what matters and loses what doesn't.

What matters is this: I learned that a small set of consistent elements, organized systematically, can represent an enormous amount of information. That meaning emerges from structure. That compression isn't about making things shorter—it's about making things systematic.

I learned this from a biology class.

I used it for everything after.

And I never once thought about the teacher, or the diagram, or whether any of it was true.

I just kept using the idea.

That's what made it real.
