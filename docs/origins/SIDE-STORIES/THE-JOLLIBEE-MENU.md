# SIDE STORY: THE JOLLIBEE MENU

The first time I ate at Jollibee, I didn't know what to expect.

It was a regular restaurant—yellow and red, the same colors as every other fast food place. The smell of fried chicken. The particular brightness of fluorescent lights over plastic trays. I was hungry, I was traveling, and Jollibee was there.

I joined the line. It moved quickly. When I reached the counter, I looked up at the menu board and felt the familiar pressure of deciding under observation. Behind the counter, someone was calling out orders—names I didn't recognize, combinations of letters and numbers that meant nothing to me.

"C1!"

A tray slid across the counter. Someone picked it up and left.

"C3!"

Another tray. Another departure.

I looked back at the menu board. Under Chickenjoy, I saw it: a small yellow label with **C1** next to a description. "2pcs Chickenjoy w/ 1 Side & Drink."

C3 was there too, though I couldn't read it from where I was standing. Somewhere in the layout, other codes existed. The crew called them out like they were obvious. The customers who heard them moved without hesitation.

I didn't know what C1 meant until I looked. But everyone else in that restaurant did.

---

That evening, I thought about the menu board differently.

The codes weren't just abbreviations. They were shortcuts into shared understanding. When someone heard "C1" called out, they didn't need the full description. They already knew. The menu board had taught them, or someone had told them, or they'd ordered C1 enough times that it had become familiar.

This is compression. Not the technical kind—not ZIP files or MP3s. Context compression. The code is small. The meaning it carries is larger. And the gap between the two is bridged by shared knowledge.

I don't know how C1 became the code for that particular combination. I don't know who decided it, or when, or why. What I know is that it works. The crew uses it. The customers respond to it. The system holds.

---

Over the next few days, I ate at Jollibee again. The same codes appeared. C1 was still there. The pattern hadn't changed.

I started noticing other things. The way regulars walked in and ordered without looking at the menu. The way the crew anticipated what people wanted. The way certain phrases seemed to carry weight beyond their literal meaning.

None of this was written down anywhere. It existed in the space between people. In the shared understanding that builds up over time, in the transactions that repeat, in the small negotiations of a busy lunch rush.

I think that's what fascinated me. Not the codes themselves, but what the codes revealed. A system of shared knowledge that made communication faster, easier, more efficient. The context doing the work that words would otherwise have to carry.

---

I should be honest: I don't know exactly when I started connecting what I observed at Jollibee to questions about Enzo. Memory doesn't preserve timelines that cleanly. It gives us fragments—the feeling of noticing something, the feeling of understanding something later.

What I know is that somewhere in my thinking, the experience of standing in that line, hearing codes called out, watching people respond without hesitation, became part of how I thought about knowledge. About how context works. About how shared understanding makes short signals meaningful.

The specific moments blur together now. Different Jollibee locations. Different trips. The menu boards blur together too. But the pattern remains: short codes representing larger contexts. Shared knowledge bridging the gap.

---

Jollibee taught me something about context compression, though I didn't know to call it that at the time.

I went for the chicken. I stayed for the observation.

That's often how it works. The lessons are there if you're looking. They hide in ordinary places—in menu codes, in call signs, in the small efficiencies that make busy restaurants function.

What looks like simplicity from the outside is actually a system. A shared understanding that took time to build. Codes that mean something because everyone agrees they mean something.

I don't remember every Jollibee I visited. But I remember the pattern.

And sometimes, when I'm thinking about how to represent complex ideas with simple identifiers, I think about C1. About what it means. About what it represents.

About what the code actually carries.
