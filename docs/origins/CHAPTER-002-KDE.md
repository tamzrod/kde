# CHAPTER-002: KDE

There came a moment when the name stopped fitting.

KDSE had served us well. It described what we were doing: building software that could help with knowledge discovery. The "S" stood for "Software," and that felt accurate for a long time.

But accuracy and truth are different things.

The software was still there. It was still being developed, improved, used. But increasingly, the conversations shifted away from what the software could do and toward what the methodology could teach. People were asking questions that had nothing to do with our specific implementation. They wanted to understand the approach. The principles. The habits of mind that made the work effective.

The questions were bigger than our runtime.

---

I remember a particular conversation that made this clear.

Someone asked us how they could apply our approach to their own project. They had different tools, different constraints, different goals. They didn't want to use our software—they wanted to understand how we thought about problems.

We started explaining. The investigations. The evidence standards. The way we structured questions before pursuing answers. The humility required to follow the evidence even when it contradicted our expectations.

As we talked, I realized something: nothing we were describing required our software. The methodology could stand alone. It could be adapted. It could inform other approaches without being tied to any particular implementation.

The software was valuable. But the software was optional.

The methodology was essential.

That distinction sounds obvious when I write it now. But at the time, it was a revelation. We had spent so long building the runtime that we hadn't fully appreciated what the runtime was building in us—and what that meant for how the work could grow.

---

The question became: what should we call something that wasn't software?

The original name carried too much. "Software" said: this is a tool. This is what you install. This is what you run. Even people who understood that we meant something more couldn't help hearing the "S" and thinking in terms of applications, features, APIs.

We talked about it for weeks. Not the name itself—we had always used KDE informally when we talked about the approach—but what the name should mean. What it should emphasize. What it should promise.

KDE. Knowledge Discovery through Engineering.

"Engineering" was deliberate. It suggested rigor, intentionality, the careful construction of systems. It suggested that this wasn't just about discovering knowledge but about building something that could help others discover knowledge too. The methodology wasn't a set of guidelines. It was a practice. A craft. Something you developed through doing, not just by reading.

The "S" was gone. And with it, the implicit promise that this was software first.

What remained was the question that had started everything: how do we discover knowledge through a structured, reliable process?

That question was bigger than any project. It was bigger than our particular implementation. And now it had a name that could grow with it.

---

The transition wasn't sudden.

For a long time, KDSE and KDE coexisted. KDSE was the runtime. KDE was the methodology. People who wanted to use our tools used KDSE. People who wanted to understand our approach learned KDE.

But even as the names distinguished themselves, they remained connected. KDSE wasn't replaced—it was contextualized. It became one implementation of the methodology. The first, maybe. The one that had grown alongside the ideas from the beginning. But no longer the only way to think about what KDE meant.

This is what we hadn't understood when we started: the methodology would outlive any particular implementation. The ideas were the thing. The runtime was how those ideas touched the world, but the ideas themselves were independent.

KDSE found its new role naturally. It became the laboratory where KDE was practiced. The place where new investigations were developed, tested, refined. The reference implementation that others could compare against when they tried their own approaches.

The software didn't become less important. It became honest about what it was.

---

I think about the name change often. The single letter.

Removing the "S" sounds trivial. A cosmetic change. A rebranding exercise. But it wasn't that at all.

It was an admission of what we had learned: that the most valuable thing we had built wasn't code. It was a way of thinking. And ways of thinking don't belong to any particular tool.

The software had taught us this. The software had led us to the methodology. But at some point, the methodology outgrew its container.

KDE is what remained when we stopped confusing the vessel with the water it carried.

KDSE continues. It runs. It executes investigations. It helps people reason through complex questions. But it no longer carries the full weight of what we're trying to do.

That weight now has a name of its own.

And that name doesn't end with a letter that could make anyone think this was ever just about software.
